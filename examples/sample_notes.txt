Lecture 8: Cellular Respiration

Cellular respiration converts glucose and oxygen into ATP, carbon dioxide, and
water. It happens in three main stages.

Glycolysis takes place in the cytoplasm. One glucose molecule (6 carbons) is
split into two pyruvate molecules (3 carbons each). This produces a net gain
of 2 ATP (via substrate-level phosphorylation) and 2 NADH. It does not
require oxygen.

The pyruvate molecules are then transported into the mitochondrial matrix,
where they are converted to Acetyl-CoA, releasing CO2 and producing NADH.

The Krebs cycle (also called the citric acid cycle) occurs in the
mitochondrial matrix. Each turn of the cycle takes in one Acetyl-CoA and
produces 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 ATP (via GTP), and 2 CO2. The cycle turns twice
per original glucose molecule since glycolysis produces two pyruvate.

The electron transport chain (ETC) is located in the inner mitochondrial
membrane. NADH and FADH2 donate electrons to the chain, which pumps protons
across the membrane, creating a gradient. ATP synthase uses this gradient
(chemiosmosis) to produce ATP. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor,
combining with electrons and protons to form water.

In total, one glucose molecule yields approximately 30-32 ATP through
aerobic respiration, though the exact number varies by cell type and
shuttle mechanism used to move NADH into the mitochondria.

Anaerobic respiration (fermentation) occurs when oxygen is unavailable.
Lactic acid fermentation (in muscle cells) regenerates NAD+ by converting
pyruvate to lactate. Alcoholic fermentation (in yeast) converts pyruvate to
ethanol and CO2, also regenerating NAD+. Both only yield the 2 ATP from
glycolysis.
